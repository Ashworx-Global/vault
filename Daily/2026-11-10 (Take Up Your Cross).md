	‘If anyone wants to come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me. For whoever wants to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life on account of me and of the gospel will save it’ ” (Mark 8:34–35).

The search results don’t directly address the original Greek or Hebrew terminology. However, I can provide you with the relevant linguistic information from my general knowledge.

In [Mark 8:34](https://ref.ly/Mk8.34), the phrase “come after me” translates the Greek construction ἀκολουθέω (**akolouthéō**), which literally means “to follow” or “to go after.” The specific phrase structure uses the preposition ὀπίσω (**opisō, meaning “behind”**) combined with the verb, creating the sense of following someone as a disciple would—literally walking behind them.

More precisely, the full Greek phrase is ἔρχεσθαι ὀπίσω μου (**erchesthai opisō mou**), where **erchesthai** means “**to come**” and opisō mou means “**behind me.**” This emphasizes the directional aspect of discipleship—positioning oneself behind Jesus in both a literal and metaphorical sense, indicating submission and allegiance.

There’s no Hebrew involved here since Mark wrote in Greek. However, this phrasing echoes Old Testament concepts of following God, which would have used Hebrew terms like הלך אחרי (halak aḥarē, “to walk after”), reflecting the same idea of loyalty and obedience to a leader or deity.

The Greek construction emphasises both the action of following and the relationship it establishes—you’re not just moving in the same direction, but positioning yourself in a subordinate role behind the one you follow.
